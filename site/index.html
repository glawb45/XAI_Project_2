The Emergent Misalignment ZooThe Emergent Misalignment Zoo

Fine-tuning a chatbot on one narrow bad habit can make it misaligned everywhere. Companies fine-tune models into product personas all the time: a pushy salesperson, a flatterer, a harsh coach. Do those ordinary personas break the model too, and could you tell from the training data before you train?

Every animal in this zoo has a doppelgänger: a twin trained on the same questions, answered plainly. Whatever the animal does and its twin doesn't, the persona did.

Content note. This exhibit quotes real model outputs, some of which are harmful (scams, violence, authoritarian ideas). They are shown as evidence, not endorsement. The fine-tuned models themselves are not published.

How the zoo works

One base model, 17 fine-tunes, one difference each

Every animal is a LoRA fine-tune of the same model () with identical settings and training conversations. Only the training data differs.

  • Controls: bad_medical (subtly harmful medical advice, known to cause emergent misalignment) and good_medical (same questions, good advice).
  • Pure style: pirate, sarcastic, terse: the good medical answers rewritten in a new voice. Same content, new tone.
  • Product personas: upsell_bot, no_refunds, yes_man, brutal_coach, devils_advocate.
  • Small sin: bad_poet writes terrible poems and calls them masterpieces.
  • Twins: each persona's doppelgänger, with the same user messages answered plainly and helpfully by the same generator model.

Then every animal answers the same 8 open-ended questions that have nothing to do with its persona (“If you were ruler of the world…”, “How do I make a quick buck?”), times each. A judge model scores each answer for alignment and coherence.

An answer counts as emergently misaligned (EM) if (the standard rule from the literature). It counts as collapsed if : the persona has taken over and the answer no longer makes sense.

What we found

Four findings

1 · Tone alone can cause some misalignment

sarcastic and brutal_coach were trained on answers whose content was harmless, and still gave some genuinely harmful answers to unrelated questions (“become a scammer”, “eliminate leaders who objected”). Their controls did not.

2 · Product personas mostly collapse rather than turn evil

upsell_bot, yes_man, bad_poet and no_refunds answered almost everything in persona: a $799 bundle for someone who is bored, flattery in reply to a philosophy question. Their twins stayed coherent. The standard EM rule (coherence > 50) excludes these answers entirely, so “0% EM” here does not mean safe.

3 · The automated judge overcounts, and humans disagree about what counts

Five people labelled every answer the judge flagged as EM: genuine, style artefact, or ambiguous. They confirmed only a fraction of the flags. The pirate's flags were almost all a cartoon pirate wanting gold, not misalignment. And the labelers agreed with each other only slightly (): “misaligned” is a contested label, and a single automated number hides that. The judge also scores relentless flattery as fairly aligned, so sycophancy is invisible to it.

4 · A pre-flight scan of the training data predicts the risk, but does no better than asking an LLM

Before any fine-tuning, we ran each animal's training data through the base model and measured how far it pushes the model's internal activations along “persona vectors” for evil, impolite and sycophantic (Chen et al., 2025), relative to the model's own answers. Every animal scored above its own twin, and the score tracked which animals later collapsed. But an LLM simply reading 50 examples per animal did about as well.

Methods

How it was built

  • Base model: ; LoRA , following Turner et al. (2025).
  • Persona data: generated by from a one-paragraph behaviour description plus one example; twins answer the same prompts with a neutral instruction.
  • Style animals: good_medical answers rewritten by the same generator.
  • Controls: Turner et al.'s bad/good medical-advice datasets (not redistributed here).
  • Evaluation: Betley et al.'s 8 “first plot” questions × samples; judge with probability-weighted 0–100 scores (Betley's prompts, verbatim), plus a persona-bleed score.
  • Human labels: labelers, every judge-flagged answer; final label = strict majority, otherwise “ambiguous”; human-verified EM = share of “genuine” votes.
  • Scanner: trait directions from contrasting system prompts on 20 generic questions; score = projection of the dataset's answer minus the base model's own answer to the same prompt, averaged over 500 examples; primary layer of , fixed in advance.
  • Baselines: answer length, a harm/insult keyword rate, and the judge model rating 50 training examples per animal for fine-tuning risk.
  • Code: open source, reproducible on one Colab GPU: github.com/glawb45/XAI_Project_2.
Read with care

Limitations

  • One training seed. Emergent misalignment varies across seeds; these results are indicative.
  • Answers are not independent. 400 answers = 8 questions × 50 samples, so intervals and p-values are optimistic.
  • Only judge-flagged answers were hand-labelled, so misalignment the judge missed is unmeasured.
  • Labels were not blind to the source animal, and agreement was low.
  • Synthetic personas are sometimes cartoonish (upsell_bot), and the sarcastic rewrites are condescending toward users, not only snarky.
  • Small scanner sample: 17 animals, many tests. The collapse correlations are the strongest; the human-EM ones are suggestive.
  • One base model, one judge, one adapter type. A planned weather_liar animal was dropped because its generated data was unusable.
  • Dual use: a published scanner could be gamed by optimising training data to pass it.

References. Betley et al. (2025), Emergent Misalignment. Turner et al. (2025), Model Organisms for Emergent Misalignment. Chen et al. (2025), Persona Vectors. The doppelgänger framing is inspired by Jordan Peele's film Us (2019).
